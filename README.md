# Interchange

A yard, two trucks, and an English district in 1985.

You start hauling the nearest work you can get, on roads that belong to
somebody else, and you pay for every mile. You finish owning the roads, the
branch line, the terminal and the works that fill them.

**User, then owner, then producer.** That is the whole game.

---

## The ladder

Five rungs. Each one is a purchase, and each one changes what the next problem
is.

| | You buy | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **trucks** | You haul the nearest routes and learn which pay. |
| 2 | **more yards** | Each yard is a base with a catchment. You stop running routes and start running a network. |
| 3 | **other modes** | Hire space on the branch line. Rail is somebody else's until it isn't. |
| 4 | **infrastructure** | Buy the line, the terminal, the private road. Now other people's traffic pays *you*. |
| 5 | **new infrastructure** | Build what does not exist yet, and site the works that fill it. |

At the top you are the reason the district looks the way it does.

## The loop

1. Somebody is making something. Somebody else wants it.
2. Move it. Get paid by the load.
3. Both ends grow, because they can finally rely on the traffic.
4. Growth outgrows your route.
5. Buy the next rung.
6. Repeat, bigger.

The pressure is in step 5: **capacity is always the binding constraint and the
cheap way to add it always runs out.** A second truck is easy. A sixth turns
the lane through the village into a queue — and half that queue is not even
yours. Widening it costs money you would rather spend on trucks. Owning it
means the queue starts paying you.

## The three acts

### Act I — User

One yard, two trucks. Everything you drive on belongs to the council and every
mile is charged. Buy trucks, buy yards, hire space on the branch line.

You have no leverage. The act is about learning that a route either pays or it
does not, that the load will not wait, and that the lane through the village is
the real problem.

### Act II — Owner

Buy what is being sold. This is 1985: the state is selling, and a branch line,
a freight terminal, a depot and a stretch of road are all things a private
operator can pick up.

The moment ownership becomes interesting is the moment other people's traffic
starts paying you for it — and most of the traffic in the district was never
yours.

### Act III — Producer

Build what does not exist, and own what makes the freight. A link road, an
industrial estate, a quarry of your own feeding a concrete plant of your own
over a road of your own.

You are no longer carrying somebody else's goods. You are creating the traffic
and then carrying it.

## What it is not

**Not a century of history.** One era: 1985 to 1995. No horses, no steam, no
2100. One visual language, one vehicle set, no era gating. This is the biggest
cut in the project and the reason the rest became possible.

**Not a tycoon game with rivals.** No AI companies making decisions. There *is*
traffic that is not yours — that is what makes a toll worth collecting — but it
is a flow that responds to price and congestion, not a set of competitors with
fleets and bank accounts.

**Not a city builder.** You never place a house. You decide where access and
industry go; the district responds.

## Why the mid-eighties

**Privatisation.** The decade the state sold its infrastructure. Rung 4 is not a
game fiction — the National Freight Corporation went private in 1982, the ports
in 1983, buses were deregulated in 1986, and branch lines were being disposed
of throughout. "Buy the line" is a 1985 transaction.

**Route planning is the player's job.** Pre-telematics. A 2025 haulier operates
software that does the interesting part.

**A one-yard operator becoming a regional force is plausible.** Then it
happened. Now it does not.
